The Word That Meant Two Different Things

Three project docs — the overview, the leftfootLabs product doc, and the deployment doc — hadn't been touched since well before the shelf model existed. Checking them meant something more specific than a re-read: comparing every real claim against what had actually shipped, line by line, rather than trusting that "mostly still true" was good enough.

A framing that was flatly wrong, not just outdated

The overview doc still described devices placed directly into rack units and validated against "a leftfootLabs kit specifically." Neither is true anymore. Devices sit on shelves, the kit itself was never placeable at all, and the kit-compatibility table that framing depended on got built, then dropped, in favor of capacity numbers living on the shelf itself. The product doc had the same problem from the other direction — it described kits as the thing that would appear in the planner as placeable, purchasable items, when the real placeable, purchasable unit turned out to be the shelf.

A claim a real incident had already overturned

The deployment doc's own gotchas list said the rollback path was unproven and needed a deliberately broken test deploy before it could be trusted. That's no longer true, and hadn't been for a few days — a real, unplanned deploy failure had already fired the rollback for real, correctly, arguably more convincingly than a staged test would have. The doc just hadn't caught up to its own project's history yet.

A conflict surfaced, not resolved

The overview doc defines a single meaning for "v1": accounts, nothing else, with an explicit reason for holding community submissions until after accounts exist — avoiding moderation problems before there's even a login system to attach them to. Four new scope docs written in the same pass got labeled "v1" too, and one of them recommends building community submissions without waiting for accounts at all. That's a real reversal of a documented decision, and it happened by scoping one thing without weighing it against something already written down. Fixing that quietly would have been worse than leaving it flagged — a decision this genuinely fifty-fifty deserves an actual answer, not a guess dressed up as an update.

Updating a doc isn't just correcting stale facts. Sometimes the most useful thing an update can do is refuse to paper over a real disagreement between two things that were both written in good faith at different times.

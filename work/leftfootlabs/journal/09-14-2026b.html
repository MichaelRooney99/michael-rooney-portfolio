Just the Faceplate

Reconciling the project docs happened the same day the actual next feature got scoped for real, and the two turned out to sharpen each other. The starting idea was broad: let someone customize a shelf around whatever devices they place on it. What it turned into, after a real back-and-forth about what's actually being sold, was much narrower and much more buildable — a standardized shelf, and a single customized part: the faceplate.

The part that doesn't need generating at all

A 10-inch rack shelf's outer dimensions are already standardized enough that generating one dynamically would be solving a problem that doesn't exist — the shelf can be a fixed, generic visual every time. The only real geometry this ever needs to produce is the thin insert with cutouts for whatever's actually placed. That's a meaningfully smaller problem than "generate an entire shelf," and it fell out of a real product conversation about what's genuinely being sold, not an engineering simplification imposed on the idea from outside.

Choosing a tool sized to the actual problem

Generating that faceplate for real — as a downloadable file, not a picture of one — meant picking a real geometry library, and the obvious-sounding choices turned out to be the wrong fit. Tools built around a full CAD kernel are designed for organic, filleted, curved mechanical parts. A faceplate is a flat plate with rectangular holes cut in it. A lighter mesh-based library whose core operation is exactly "cut a shape out of a plate" fits the real problem directly, instead of bringing a much heavier tool to a job that never needed it.

A measurement that didn't need taking

Fitting a keystone jack into a faceplate needs a real width and height, and the instinct was to treat that as another real-world measurement waiting to happen. It isn't — keystone jacks are built to a single, tightly standardized real dimension precisely so any connector type snaps into the same cutout regardless of manufacturer. Multiple independent sources converged on the same number within normal tolerance, the same kind of convergence this project already trusted once before for the rack-width standard itself. Worth checking rather than assuming, and this time checking meant confirming a real fact already existed rather than going and creating one.

Not every open question is a research gap. Some of them are settled facts nobody's looked up yet.

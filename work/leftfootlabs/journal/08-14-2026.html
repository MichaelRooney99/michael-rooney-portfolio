Building the Thing Before the Store

leftfootLabs has a domain now and a design that's been through enough Blender iterations to actually print well — bolted flanges, slide-in side panels, a footprint mark on the foot that's more than just decoration. What it doesn't have yet is a store, and that's deliberate. Before anyone can buy a rack kit, I want a tool that proves the kit is worth buying in the first place.

That tool is the Rack Planner — an interactive tool for laying out a homelab rack, checking that everything actually fits, and estimating how long a UPS keeps it running under real load. It's not a storefront feature bolted onto a shop. It's the thing that comes first, on purpose, because a "does this fit" tool is more useful to a stranger deciding whether to trust a small print shop than a product page ever will be.

Why the ordering matters

It would be easy to treat the planner as a nice-to-have and just stand up a Shopify store with photos of the kit. That's the faster path to something that looks finished. It's also the weaker one. A planner that lets someone drag their actual gear into a rack layout and see a leftfootLabs kit genuinely fit — before they've spent a dollar — does more to earn trust than any amount of product copy. The store can come later, once there's something worth linking a "buy" button to.

There's a second reason, less about the product and more about what I actually want out of building it: the planner forces engineering ground the capstone never touched. Accounts, saved state, a real constraint problem in getting devices to fit correctly in a rack elevation — not just reading live data and displaying it, but modeling something and storing what a user did with it. Sticking with the same React/TypeScript/Express stack the capstone already proved out was a deliberate choice too — the point isn't to prove I can learn a new framework, it's to get real repetition on the one I just spent months learning, while the actual novelty comes from the problem itself.

What "before the storefront" actually means

Concretely: a rack elevation you can place real devices into, a power budget that adds up what's plugged in, and a runtime estimate for how long a UPS holds that load — built from actual discharge behavior pulled off my own SX950U rather than a generic linear formula, because if I'm going to claim a number means something, I want to have actually measured it. Shareable snapshot links first, accounts after, kept deliberately minimal — a username and a properly hashed password, nothing more collected than what's needed. No storefront integration at all yet. That's explicitly staged for later, once there's a product worth selling through one.

Right now this is still on paper — the shape of the thing is scoped, the open decisions are closed, and the first real session is data collection, not code: pulling a real discharge curve off the UPS before the runtime estimator gets written at all. Nothing here is built yet. This entry is the marker for why it's getting built in this order, so that six months from now, if the sequencing looks strange out of context, there's a record of it not being an accident.

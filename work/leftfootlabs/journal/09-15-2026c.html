A Missing Width Doesn't Cost You Everything

The standardized-shelf simplification cleared the way for the actual physical fit check a shelf's face needs: does everything placed on it fit, with real breathing room between neighbors and from the shelf's own edges. One real question had been left open on purpose rather than guessed at going in — what happens when an item's own width isn't measured, which today is every real device in the catalog.

A decision made at the level of the check, not the item

The easy version of this would have picked one of two extremes: block anything with an unmeasured width from being validated at all, or ignore the whole question and let it through. Neither was actually right. Whether a device's left edge sits far enough from the shelf's own edge only depends on where it's positioned — not how wide it is — so that check stays real and meaningful even when the width itself is a genuine unknown. Only the checks that actually need the width — does it run past the far edge, is it too close to its neighbor — get skipped for that specific item, not the item as a whole.

Proof, not just agreement

The same real discipline from when this validation logic first got duplicated between the server and the browser applied again here: a single scenario, run through both real implementations against identical data, deliberately provoking three different real failure kinds at once — a device too close to the edge, two items crowded together, and a third device that plain doesn't fit at all. Comparing the two outputs directly confirmed they agree byte for byte, not just that they were written to look alike.

A rule can be strict about what it checks without being strict about when it's allowed to check anything at all. Those are two different kinds of caution, and conflating them would have made the feature stricter than the real data could actually support.

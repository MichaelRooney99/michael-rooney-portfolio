A Shelf That Already Sat Exactly at the Line

The shelf restructuring from the day before left two checks still to build on top of it: does a shelf actually clip into a standard 10-inch rack, and does whatever's placed on it fit that shelf's real depth and weight limits. Neither depends on the other, so both landed in the same pass — and one of them got a genuinely useful assist from data that was already sitting in the seed file.

A boundary test that didn't need inventing

Testing a tolerance check properly means testing the exact boundary, not just comfortably inside or clearly outside it — a shelf measured at precisely the standard width plus its tolerance should still pass, not fail on a technicality. Rather than fabricate a shelf at exactly that number, one of the six real shelves seeded a session earlier turned out to already sit there: measured at 252mm against a 254mm ±2mm standard, a delta of exactly 2mm. Real measured hardware landing precisely on the boundary that mattered, without anyone arranging it that way, is a small thing, but it's the kind of small thing that's worth noticing instead of walking past.

A running total, not a per-device number

The capacity check splits depth and weight on purpose, and they're genuinely different kinds of check. Depth is a per-device question — does this one thing fit, independent of whatever else is on the shelf. Weight is a running total across everything on that shelf, since a weight limit is a property of the shelf itself, not of any single item on it. The real test for that distinction is two devices that are each individually fine but combine to exceed the limit together — and specifically checking that only the device which actually tips the total over gets flagged, not the first one that happened to be placed.

A shelf with an unmeasured weight capacity skips the weight check entirely, rather than failing closed or guessing a number to compare against. A missing number isn't the same thing as zero, and treating it as either extreme would be worse than just being honest that the capacity isn't known yet.

Checked against real shelves, not just fixtures

The last real check wasn't a unit test at all: taking all six real seeded shelves — the ones with genuinely independent origins this whole shelf model exists to support — and placing every one of them into a single ten-unit layout, then running the actual validation function against that real data end to end. Clean pass, zero errors, across collision, height, and width checks at once. A green test suite proves the logic is correct in isolation; running it against the real seeded catalog is what confirms it's correct for the thing it's actually for.

Forty-three of forty-three tests passing closes out the full arc that started with reframing a kit-compatibility feature into a real shelf model two days earlier — foundation, restructuring, and both independent checks, each verified against something real rather than assumed correct from a clean diff.

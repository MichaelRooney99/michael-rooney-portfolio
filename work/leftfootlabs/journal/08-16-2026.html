What the First Test Print Actually Taught Me

First real print of the rack came back with two honest problems. The post's top spigot printed as basically its own separate piece, barely touching the post underneath it. And the corner and beam joints had generated support material somewhere I couldn't reach it — inside a fully enclosed hollow tube, with no way in after the fact except cutting the part open.

Both came from the same root cause once I actually understood it: the post was a hollow tube, and the spigot was a solid cap sitting directly on top of that hollow interior. Printing that means bridging solid material across open air — printing a layer with nothing underneath it, relying on the material stiffening fast enough not to sag before the next layer locks it in. PETG doesn't bridge span like that reliably, so the spigot layer partially failed, and the slicer tried to compensate with supports it had no way to place anywhere useful, since the only space available was sealed on every side.

The chamfer that solved one thing and broke another

First fix attempt was a chamfered "house" profile on the joint sockets and spigots — a peaked cross-section instead of a flat-topped square, so the printer never has to bridge more than a couple millimeters at any layer near the top. That part worked. The bridging problem genuinely went away.

What I didn't account for: the heat-insert bore for each joint sits at a fixed height, and once the surrounding material stopped being a plain square and started sloping away at 45 degrees, part of that bore's own wall went from solid material to open chamfer. The insert ended up held in by less than half its own circumference. Solved the print problem, broke the actual joint. Reverted the chamfer back to plain squares and I'm handling the bridging the same way I'm handling it on the post now — through slicer settings, not geometry.

What actually fixed the post

The real fix for the post wasn't a shape change at all — it was making it solid. Turns out the shelf-mount bores had a second, separate problem hiding under the bridging issue: an 8.4mm-deep bore drilled into a wall that was only 3mm thick. Every one of those "blind" holes was never actually blind — it was punching straight through into the hollow interior every time. A solid post gives every bore, all the way down the post's length, real material to stop in. Costs more filament and more print time than the hollow version did. That's the trade being made here on purpose, not an oversight.

Small things that add up to real assembly

A few smaller fixes came out of actually looking at printed geometry from angles I hadn't checked before. The beam's bolt clearance hole was drilled all the way through both walls of the joint, including a thin inner wall right next to the socket that never needed a hole at all — the heat insert lives in the corner piece, not the beam, so the beam only ever needed a clean pass-through on the outside face. And every insert bore that's meant to be accessed during assembly now consistently opens toward the outside of a joint instead of into the tight inside corner — small thing, but it's the difference between reaching a bolt with your fingers and fighting for it.

Last piece from tonight: the bottom corner's accessory cavity — reserved for a future foot attachment — now has bolt access from the two outer faces that don't already have a beam spigot. Positioned at the same relative depth every other pin in this design uses, so it's a real consistent convention and not a coincidence. Same corner piece also picked up a second insert bore on its post-connecting stub, mirroring the one it already had, so that connection point has two usable directions instead of one — a small bit of headroom for whatever this part ends up doing besides just holding a post upright.

Test print of this round is running now. Whatever it shows, it'll show something real — that's the only kind of feedback that's mattered so far.

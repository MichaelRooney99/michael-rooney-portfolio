N10-009 Study Notes: Review & What's Next

Part five of five. Four entries of concept, implementation, security, operations, and troubleshooting. This one is the consolidation pass — where everything gets compressed back down to what matters most for the final stretch before the exam, and where the path forward starts to take shape.

If you've been following this series as a study companion, this is the entry to read twice. First time through to identify your gaps. Second time to confirm you've closed them.

Domain Weights — Where the Exam Lives

The N10-009 exam is 90 questions, 90 minutes, passing score 720 out of 900. The domain weights aren't just organizational — they tell you where to concentrate your final week.

DomainWeightEntry
1.0 Networking Concepts23%Part 1
2.0 Network Implementation19%Part 2
3.0 Network Operations17%Part 3
4.0 Network Security20%Part 3
5.0 Network Troubleshooting21%Part 4

Domains 4 and 5 together are 41% of the exam. Domain 1 is another 23%. Those three areas alone account for nearly two-thirds of the total score. That's where to spend the last week.

High-Priority Recall — Domain 1

The OSI model layers in order, and what lives at each one. The difference between TCP and UDP and which protocols use which. Port numbers — the full common list, in both directions (port to protocol, protocol to port). Private IPv4 ranges by heart. Subnet math: given a /24, /25, /26, /27, know the number of hosts, the network address format, and the broadcast address immediately. IPv6 address types and the loopback. DNS record types. The DORA sequence for DHCP. What NAT does and why private addresses need it.

The subnetting piece is the one that requires drilling, not just reading. Use ExamCompass or a subnetting worksheet and do it until it's automatic. On exam day, time pressure and question framing will try to slow you down. Mechanical comfort with the math removes that variable.

High-Priority Recall — Domain 2

How a switch builds its MAC address table and makes forwarding decisions. What a hub does differently and why it's inferior. VLAN concepts: access ports versus trunk ports, 802.1Q tagging, inter-VLAN routing requiring a Layer 3 device. What STP does and what problem it solves — broadcast storms, root bridge election, blocked ports. The 802.11 wireless standards: which bands each uses, approximate maximum throughputs, and the key distinguishing features. WPA2 versus WPA3 and why WEP is off the table. What IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS each describe.

High-Priority Recall — Domains 3 and 4

Stateless versus stateful versus NGFW — what each inspects and what it can't. IDS versus IPS — the key distinction is passive detection versus active prevention. VPN tunnel types and the protocols behind them. The AAA framework — what each letter means and which protocol implements it (RADIUS, TACACS+). SNMP versions and the critical difference between v2c and v3. Syslog severity levels, 0–7, in order. What a FHRP does and how HSRP and VRRP differ. Documentation types: physical diagrams, logical diagrams, IPAM. What change management is for and why a rollback plan matters.

High-Priority Recall — Domain 5

The seven-step troubleshooting methodology, in order, without looking it up. What each command-line tool does: ping, traceroute, nslookup, dig, netstat, nmap, iperf, Wireshark. The layer-by-layer diagnostic approach and which layer each tool addresses. Common failure patterns: IP conflict, wrong default gateway, DNS failure, duplex mismatch, VLAN misconfiguration, physical layer issues. The habit of forming a theory before making a change — and documenting what you did after.

Final Week Strategy

Practice exams are the primary tool at this point, not new content. Each practice exam does two things: it surfaces knowledge gaps, and it builds the endurance to sustain focus for 90 minutes under time pressure. Professor Messer's practice exams are the closest to the actual exam style. Jason Dion's are higher volume and useful for drilling specific domains.

After each practice exam, review every wrong answer — not just the correct answer, but why the other choices were wrong. The exam uses distractor answers that are plausible enough to catch you if your understanding is shallow. Understanding why the wrong answers are wrong builds the same mental model that produces the right answer on questions you haven't seen.

The night before: nothing new. Review the port list and the OSI layers. Sleep. The exam is 90 minutes — it's not a marathon of endurance, it's a sprint of recall. Rest matters more than a last-minute cram at that point.

The goal isn't to feel ready. The goal is to pass. Those aren't the same thing. Feeling ready is a subjective state that's never quite satisfied. Passing is a number: 720 out of 900. Practice exam scores tell you whether you're there, not your confidence level.

What the Homelab Built — Honest Accounting

Before moving to what comes next, it's worth being direct about what the homelab actually prepared versus what the studying filled in.

The lab built genuine intuition for: subnetting and IP address management (having managed a static allocation table across 20+ addresses for months). DNS and how resolution flows (having watched nslookup and dig behavior across a multi-service environment). VPN overlay networking (Tailscale's behavior under various routing conditions, including failure modes). Monitoring architecture — what metrics to collect, what thresholds to set, the difference between availability and performance data. Troubleshooting methodology — not by name, but by practice across a dozen debugging sessions that each required forming a theory, testing it, and moving on when it was wrong. Power and redundancy at a systems level.

The studying filled in: precise terminology the exam expects. Protocol specifics at a level the lab doesn't require — OSPF versus RIP versus BGP conceptually, SNMPv3 authentication modes, TACACS+ versus RADIUS at the architectural level. Wireless standards, which the lab has but doesn't require engineering-level understanding of. The formal troubleshooting methodology as a named, ordered process. Port numbers across the full list, not just the ones that came up in practice.

The combination is what makes both worth doing. The intuition makes the terminology land somewhere real. The terminology gives the intuition precision. Neither one alone is the full picture.

What's Next — CCNA

Network+ is a proof of foundation. CCNA — Cisco Certified Network Associate — is where the depth begins. The exam (200-301) covers everything Network+ does and goes substantially further: OSPF configuration, STP behavior and tuning, ACL syntax, switch security features, HSRP and FHRP in practice, wireless LAN controller configuration, SD-WAN, network automation with Python and REST APIs. The goal is to have it done by end of summer.

That's an ambitious timeline for a meaningfully harder exam. The plan is to treat it the same way: structured domain-by-domain study, practice questions throughout rather than at the end, and the lab as the place where abstract configurations become concrete. CCNA will require hands-on CLI practice — Cisco's Packet Tracer provides a free simulation environment, and Cisco DevNet has lab exercises. The lab topology here maps reasonably well to CCNA scenarios: a multi-node environment with a managed switch, VLAN segmentation, and a firewall at the edge provides most of the physical structure.

JNCIA-Junos (Juniper's entry-level cert) is also on the roadmap. Given that the network engineering team the April conversation pointed toward is moving away from Cisco infrastructure toward Juniper and HP, JNCIA may be more immediately relevant than CCNA for that specific opportunity. Both are on the list. The order will depend on how the summer goes.

A Note for Anyone Following Along

If you started this series because you're also working toward Network+, a few honest notes from the other side of the studying.

Build something first if you can. Not because it makes the exam easier — the exam will still ask about things you've never personally configured. But because having a mental image of a real network, even a small one, makes abstract concepts land differently. When I read about VLAN trunking, I see port 8 on the Netgear switch carrying tagged traffic to OPNsense. That picture makes the concept stick in a way that a diagram in a textbook doesn't.

Do the practice exams earlier than you think you need to. Most people treat practice exams as a final check. They're more useful as a diagnostic tool throughout — each one tells you where to spend the next study session. Doing your first practice exam in the final week means you don't have time to act on what it tells you.

Write things down. Notes, a blog, a journal, a doc no one ever reads. The act of writing a concept out forces you to find the edges of your understanding. Wherever you stall or hedge or reach for a vague phrase — that's the gap. This series exists partly because writing it revealed several of those gaps before the exam did.

The certification isn't the destination. It's a credential that opens a specific door. What you actually know matters more and longer than what the cert says. Study for understanding. The passing score will follow.

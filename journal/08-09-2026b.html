Optimizing for an Audience That Doesn't Click

Spent part of today wiring structured data into this site — a Person schema block on the homepage, a batch-scripted BlogPosting block on every journal entry. Small, mechanical changes. But doing them forced a question I'd been fuzzy on for a while: what is SEO actually optimizing for, and is "AEO" a real second thing or just a rebrand chasing a trend cycle?

What SEO has always quietly assumed

Traditional SEO optimizes for one specific behavior: a person types a query, gets a results page, and clicks a link. Every classic lever — title tags, meta descriptions, canonical URLs, a submitted sitemap — exists to influence two things: whether your page shows up in that list, and whether it's compelling enough to earn the click once it does. The click is the whole model. Rank high, write a description that sells the click, done.

That assumption held up for two decades because there wasn't really another way to get information out of a search engine. You typed words, you got ten blue links, you clicked one. The page you landed on was the answer.

Where the model breaks

AI-powered search doesn't work that way anymore. Ask an AI answer engine a question and it doesn't hand you ten links — it reads several sources, synthesizes an answer, and shows you the answer directly, sometimes with a citation, sometimes without one. The page never gets visited. The "result" is a paragraph the engine wrote after reading your page, not your page itself.

That's the actual distinction between SEO and AEO, and it's more specific than "SEO but for robots." SEO optimizes for being chosen from a list. AEO optimizes for being quoted inside an answer you'll never see a click for. Same underlying technical foundation — crawlability, structured data, a site that isn't actively hostile to parsing — but a different finish line. A page can rank beautifully and still never get pulled into a synthesized answer, because ranking and being extractable turned out to be two different properties of a page, not one.

What that means for something as small as this site

It's tempting to treat AEO as enterprise-scale marketing noise — irrelevant to a portfolio site with an audience of recruiters and one instructor. Mostly, that's true. Nobody's asking an AI model to recommend a front-end developer the way they'd ask it to compare CRM software. But the underlying practices aren't wasted even at this scale, because they're really just entity clarity — making it unambiguous who this site is about and what each page is, in a format a machine can parse without guessing.

The Person schema does that at the site level — one declaration that "Michael Rooney," this domain, this GitHub, this LinkedIn, are all the same entity, rather than leaving that inference to prose parsing. The BlogPosting schema does the same thing per entry — headline, author, publish date, description, all in a format that doesn't require an engine to reconstruct structure from a wall of HTML. Whether or not either one ever gets cited by an AI answer engine, they cost almost nothing to add and they make the site's own shape more explicit — to a crawler, and honestly to a human skimming the source too.

The part that's still just SEO

One thing that didn't change: the actual writing still has to be worth surfacing. Schema tells a machine what a page is. It says nothing about whether the content is any good. Structured data can make a mediocre page easier to find and easier to misquote — it can't make it worth quoting. That part's still just... writing something true and clear enough that someone else, human or model, thinks it's worth repeating.

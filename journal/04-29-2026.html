April 29, 2026 Learning

The Call

I had the Zoom call this morning. The one I've been working toward for months — a conversation with a network engineering manager at my employer about what it would take to transition into IT. I spent the better part of yesterday evening and this morning preparing for it. Running through my opener. Writing down questions. Reminding myself of everything I've built and learned so I wouldn't forget half of it the moment someone asked.

I was nervous. Genuinely, uncomfortably nervous. The kind where you rehearse sentences in the shower and second-guess every word choice. I know what I've built. I know what I've studied. None of that stopped the anxiety from showing up right on schedule.

How it actually went

He was relaxed. Casual, even. Within the first thirty seconds the dynamic shifted from what I'd built it up to be in my head — a high-stakes evaluation — to what it actually was: two people having an honest conversation about networking, careers, and what a path forward might look like. The anxiety didn't disappear entirely but it found its place. Background noise instead of the whole room.

He described what his team manages. The scale is significant — network infrastructure across hundreds of store locations, WAN connectivity, switching, routing, and the operational complexity that comes with keeping that many locations consistently reachable. He talked about where the team is headed — more automation, more proactive response to events that impact store connectivity rather than reactive firefighting. That part landed. It's exactly the kind of work I find compelling.

What I took away

He was direct about certifications. Network+ as a foundation — which I'm already close to exam-ready on. CCNA for the depth of networking knowledge it requires. And an interesting note: the team is likely moving away from Cisco toward Juniper and HP infrastructure, which means the JNCIA-Junos certification — Juniper's entry-level networking cert — may be more directly relevant than CCNA in the near term. I hadn't heard of JNCIA before this morning. I started reading about it before lunch.

The automation conversation stuck with me the most. The team's goal of automating responses to network events is exactly where Ansible lives — the tool that's been on my homelab roadmap for months without me having prioritized it yet. That changes now. Ansible moves to the top of the list. Building playbooks in my homelab environment to automate configuration management across my nodes is directly applicable to what they're working toward.

Sometimes the most useful thing a conversation can do is tell you exactly where to point your energy next. This one did that.

The path in — honest picture

He was candid about the entry point. Internships are the standard path into the IT team, and internal candidates have a genuine advantage over external applicants — that part was encouraging. The logistics, though, are something I need to work through carefully. The internship is in-person, a significant distance from where I live, and capped at around 24 hours a week. That's a real constraint that requires honest conversations with HR and with myself before I can commit to anything.

He also mentioned the possibility of shadowing — a day or a week just observing how the team operates. I'd take that without hesitation. No logistics complexity, no financial calculation required. Just the chance to see what the work actually looks like before making any larger decisions. I mentioned it in my follow-up email.

Where this leaves me

The call ended open. No next steps defined, no commitment made on either side. That's appropriate given the considerations involved. What I have now that I didn't have yesterday morning is a clear picture — the team, the work, the certifications that matter, the path in, and the honest constraints that come with it.

The roadmap from here is sharper than it was. Network+ exam scheduled soon. JNCIA-Junos on the research list. Ansible moved from "eventually" to "now." HR conversation about internship logistics on the to-do list. And a relationship with someone in the team who knows my name, has read this journal, and took an hour out of his morning to give me a real picture of what the path looks like.

That's not nothing. That's actually quite a lot.

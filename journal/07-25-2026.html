What a Scraper Actually Is, and How Mine Works

Birthday, and I wanted to spend part of it on something that was purely curiosity rather than a to-do list item. "Scraper" is a word I use constantly without ever having written down what it actually means — so today's the day, and I've already got a real one sitting in my own codebase to use as the example instead of reaching for a toy demo.

What a scraper actually is

The word conjures a very specific image — a script pulling prices off a shopping site, or a bot indexing the web — but that's the most famous instance of the pattern, not the whole definition. The general shape is much broader: a scraper is anything that automatically checks a source of data on its own schedule and pulls out a specific piece of information, without a person manually visiting that source each time.

Prometheus is a scraper. It's not usually described that way in casual conversation, but the word is right there in its own documentation — it "scrapes" metrics endpoints on an interval. The mental leap that made the rest of this actually click: a scraper isn't defined by parsing HTML. It's defined by the pattern underneath — fetch, extract, repeat, on a schedule nobody has to trigger by hand.

What every scraper actually needs

Strip away whatever's being scraped and there are really only four pieces, every time:

A trigger. Something that decides when to check — a timer firing every N minutes, a cron schedule, or an event (a webhook, a file change).

A fetch step. Actually reaching the source — a plain HTTP request for anything that exposes structured data, or a full browser automation tool when the content only exists after client-side JavaScript runs and there's no clean API to ask instead.

An extraction step. Pulling the specific thing out of whatever came back — parsing HTML for a price, or deriving "this service is degraded" from a JSON field that's really just a status code.

Somewhere to put the result. Optional in the most trivial case, load-bearing in almost every real one — without this, a scraper is just a fancy way to check something once. The storage step is what turns "checked it" into "here's a history of what it's been."

What they're actually used for

Search engines are the largest-scale version — the entire business of indexing the web is a scraper run at a civilization scale. Price comparison and shopping tools are the most commonly cited example. Security tooling scrapes for exposed secrets and broken links. Archival projects like the Wayback Machine scrape snapshots of pages over time. And monitoring and observability — Prometheus, uptime checkers, the thing I actually built — scrape internal systems instead of public websites, on a schedule measured in seconds or minutes instead of once a day.

How one's actually implemented — the one I already have

My homelab status page has had real 90-day uptime history for two of its four categories for a while now, because Prometheus already scrapes those two independently. The other two — Proxmox API and Zabbix — never had that, because neither one has anything that scrapes them on its own. So a few sessions back, I built one.

proxy/src/poller.ts is a small background job that starts the moment the Express proxy comes up. The trigger is the simplest possible one — setInterval, firing every 15 minutes. The fetch step doesn't even talk to Proxmox or Zabbix directly; it calls the proxy's own already-existing /proxmox and /zabbix routes over localhost, the same routes the browser already uses — one source of truth for "how to reach these systems," reused instead of duplicated. The extraction step is a small, deliberately minimal function that turns whatever came back into one of three words: operational, degraded, or outage. And the storage step is proxy/src/db.ts, writing each result into a SQLite database using Node's own built-in node:sqlite module — no new dependency, nothing to install, just a standard library feature that happened to exist already.

That's genuinely the whole shape of it. Nothing about a "real" scraper requires it to be large or clever — the four pieces above, wired together, on a timer. The interesting engineering decisions weren't in any single piece; they were in the seams between them — how often to check, what to do with a result that doesn't cleanly map to one of three states, and how to keep 90 days of history without the database growing forever. All small decisions, made one at a time, that add up to something that quietly runs in the background and just works.

Where this leaves me

A word I'd used a hundred times finally has an actual definition behind it instead of a vague picture of a script grabbing HTML — and it turns out I'd already built one of my own without thinking of it in those terms. Good way to spend part of a birthday: learning that something you already did has a name.

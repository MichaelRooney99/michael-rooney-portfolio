The Fleet Got Slower Overnight, and Nothing Was Wrong

Checked the status page's own service-detail charts out of simple habit and saw something that looked genuinely alarming — every monitored node's response time had roughly doubled or tripled, holding steady at the new, worse number instead of spiking and recovering. My first assumption was that some Ansible work from the day before had broken something on the network. It hadn't. What actually happened took seven separate checks to find, and every one of those checks mattered — skipping any of them would have led somewhere wrong.

Ruling out the obvious first suspect

Timed the actual application path directly — the proxy talking to the Proxmox API, the proxy talking to its own internal route. Both came back under 12 milliseconds. Whatever was slow, it wasn't the app itself.

Finding out what the number even was

The chart showing "response time" turned out not to measure a network round-trip at all — for this particular category of service, it was Prometheus's own scrape-duration metric, how long the monitoring system itself took to collect data from each machine. A completely different thing than what I assumed I was looking at, and worth actually confirming rather than guessing, because every theory downstream of a wrong assumption here would have been chasing the wrong problem entirely.

The clue that broke the first theory

My working theory was a routing problem specific to one isolated part of the network. One machine on that isolated segment was supposed to be the control case — unaffected, proving the theory. It wasn't unaffected. It showed the exact same jump as everything else. That one data point killed the theory outright, which was the right outcome — better to have a theory die cleanly against real evidence than to quietly ignore the one result that didn't fit.

Checking whether the collector itself was struggling

If every target looks slower to the thing measuring them, the measuring tool itself is worth checking. It wasn't — near-zero CPU usage, comfortable memory, nothing under any real load. Ruled out.

The graph that actually pinned it down

Pulling up the monitoring system's own historical graph showed something genuinely useful — not a gradual drift, a clean, sudden step at one exact timestamp, then a flat plateau afterward. A discrete jump at a known moment is a different kind of clue than "things have felt off lately" — it points at a specific event, not a vague trend, and a specific event can be cross-referenced against exactly what was happening at that moment.

What was happening at that moment: real infrastructure automation work, run against every affected machine at once. That timing match, on its own, wasn't proof — but it was specific enough to actually test.

Confirming the bottleneck at the actual source

Timed the raw metrics endpoint directly on three separate machines, bypassing the collector and the network entirely. All three landed within a few milliseconds of each other, and matched almost exactly what the collector had been recording. That's the moment this stopped being a theory — the slowdown was real, measurable, and located precisely: on the machines themselves, in the collection step, not anywhere upstream.

The actual mechanism

One task in that automation, designed to run exactly once per machine ever, had never actually fired before — gated behind a marker file that hadn't existed on any of these hosts until that exact session. Running it for the first time loaded additional hardware-sensor kernel modules. A monitoring flag that had been quietly enabled for weeks — reading temperature and fan sensors — suddenly had real sensor files to read that it didn't have access to before. More data per collection cycle, more time per cycle. Simple, once you can see it. Not visible at all from three levels up, which is exactly why each of the checks above had to happen.

Trusting the fix meant checking the data, not just the timing

Confirming the mechanism wasn't the end of it. Unattended hardware-sensor detection across six different machines with different hardware is exactly the kind of operation that can misread a chip and quietly report garbage that still "looks successful." Explaining why the numbers changed doesn't prove the new numbers are actually correct — those are two separate claims, and only checking the first one would have left a real gap. Pulled the actual sensor readings from every affected machine and checked them by eye: believable temperatures, sensible spread between readings, nothing reporting an impossible value. Two small, cosmetic oddities showed up — a placeholder value in an unused firmware field, and one particular sensor reading an identical number on every machine — and both turned out to be well-known driver/firmware quirks, not something specific to any one box, confirmed by the fact that they appeared identically everywhere rather than on just one.

What actually made this findable

Nothing about this investigation relied on a lucky guess. Every step either confirmed something or ruled something out, in order, and the theory that turned out to be right wasn't the first one tried — it only survived because the earlier, more obvious theories got tested against real data and failed. A genuinely alarming-looking symptom — an entire fleet apparently degrading at once — turned out to be a real, permanent, and completely benign side effect: the infrastructure is now actually reporting hardware health data it never exposed before. That's not a bug fixed. It's a new capability, temporarily disguised as a regression, until it got checked properly.

May 28, 2026 Learning Certs

Going Dark for a Bit

The voucher is purchased. The exam is coming up soon. If you've been following along here and things suddenly go quiet — no new homelab builds, no Ansible writeups, no infrastructure rabbit holes — this is why. For the next stretch, the lab takes a back seat. Network+ is the focus.

I wanted to write this down before going heads-down, partly for accountability and partly because I think the study process is worth documenting the same way everything else here gets documented. If you're working toward Network+ yourself, or just curious what the preparation looks like from someone who came in through the homelab side of things, follow along.

Where the lab stands

The timing feels right to pause on new builds. The lab is in a stable, well-monitored state — probably the best shape it's been in. Before going dark, here's a snapshot of what's running.

Five Proxmox nodes across a 10.10.10.0/24 subnet, managed behind an OPNsense gateway with Unbound DNS and a Cloudflare wildcard cert covering every internal service. Node 0 handles network services. Ankhh is the primary workload node and UPS master — the CyberPower SX950U connects directly to the host via USB, running NUT in netserver mode with four slave nodes monitoring it over the network. Shasta hosts the Docker stack and the Ansible control node. Murloc runs productivity services. PBS handles nightly backups on a dedicated bare metal box with 30-day retention.

The observability layer is fully built out. Prometheus scrapes all five nodes via node_exporter, pulls Proxmox cluster metrics through pve_exporter, and collects UPS data via nut_exporter — all feeding into Grafana dashboards and a live alerting ruleset for node availability, UPS battery state, and shutdown thresholds. Zabbix runs alongside it on Shasta for infrastructure state monitoring and SNMP polling of the Netgear GS308E switch. The two tools cover different things and don't overlap in a way that causes noise.

Ansible is a permanent part of the infrastructure now — a dedicated LXC on Shasta with six active playbooks enforcing consistent state across all five nodes. Audit, baseline packages, node_exporter version pinning, NUT master and slave configs, rolling updates. Configuration drift is what Ansible exists to prevent and it's doing its job. Vaultwarden handles credentials with three independent access paths so it survives any combination of NPM or OPNsense going down. And there's now an interactive topology map of the whole thing running on Murloc — which, if I'm being honest, was a completely optional project that I built mostly because I wanted to see it all laid out.

The lab didn't slow down because it's finished. It slowed down because there's something more important to do right now.

Why Network+ right now

Back in April, I had a Zoom call with a network engineering manager at my employer — the one I'd been building toward for months. He was direct about the certification path: Network+ as a foundation, then CCNA, with JNCIA-Junos worth a serious look given the team's infrastructure direction. That conversation moved Network+ from "something I'll get to" to the specific, scheduled thing I'm doing right now. The voucher being purchased is the concrete form of that.

The honest picture is that the lab has already taught me a significant chunk of what Network+ tests. Routing across a five-node subnet. Configuring Tailscale as a mesh VPN overlay. Troubleshooting why a NUT slave couldn't reach the master on port 3493. Understanding the difference between Zabbix's availability monitoring and Prometheus's time-series metrics — and when each one is the right tool. Debugging why SNMP polling on the switch wasn't returning data and tracing it back to a community string mismatch. All of it is applied networking. The cert formalizes what the lab has been teaching in practice.

Building something and being asked to explain it from first principles are different skills. The exam tests the second one.

The study plan

The N10-009 exam covers five domains: Networking Concepts (23%), Network Implementation (19%), Network Operations (17%), Network Security (20%), and Network Troubleshooting (21%). Security and Troubleshooting together account for nearly half the exam, which shapes where the time goes.

Phase Focus Topics
Week 1 Networking Concepts OSI model, TCP/IP stack, ports & protocols, IPv4/IPv6 addressing, subnetting, CIDR, NAT, DNS, DHCP, routing fundamentals
Week 2 Implementation & Infrastructure Ethernet, switching, VLANs, STP, wireless standards (802.11), WAN technologies, cloud concepts, virtualization networking
Week 3 Security & Operations Firewalls, IDS/IPS, VPNs, AAA, network hardening, monitoring & logging, SNMP, NetFlow, documentation, high availability
Week 4 Troubleshooting & Review Troubleshooting methodology, tools (ping, traceroute, nslookup, netstat, Wireshark), practice exams, full domain review, weak-spot targeting

The daily rhythm: new material in the morning — video content or reading, one objective at a time. Active recall in the afternoon — flash cards, practice questions on that day's topics, no passive re-reading. Evenings are for connecting concepts back to something real in the lab. That last part matters. If I can trace a concept to a configuration I've actually touched, it sticks in a way that abstract definitions don't.

Where the homelab connects

More than I expected, honestly. A few specific mappings worth naming:

The entire lab runs on a single 10.10.10.0/24 subnet with a separate isolated VLAN 20 at 10.10.20.0/24 for lab and IoT devices, trunked through the managed switch to OPNsense. That's subnetting, CIDR notation, VLAN segmentation, and inter-VLAN routing — all on the exam, all already in production. The Netgear GS308EP runs SNMP v2c, which Zabbix polls for port state and traffic counters. SNMP is a named exam objective. I've already debugged a live community string mismatch on it.

Tailscale is a mesh VPN overlay — exit nodes, subnet routing, the distinction between direct connections and relay paths. VPN tunneling is testable material and I've already broken it once in a way that taught me exactly how the routing works. NginX Proxy Manager sitting in front of every internal service is a practical exercise in reverse proxying, SSL termination, and understanding what a DNS name actually resolves to at the network layer.

And the Zabbix and Prometheus deployments between them cover nearly every monitoring and logging concept the exam names: SNMP polling, threshold-based alerting, time-series data collection, log aggregation, the difference between availability checks and performance metrics. I built both of those stacks from scratch and debugged both of them when they didn't work. That's the troubleshooting methodology the exam describes — I just didn't have the vocabulary for it at the time.

The lab didn't give me the vocabulary the exam expects. The studying does that. But the lab gave me the intuition that makes the vocabulary land somewhere real rather than somewhere abstract.

Resources

Keeping the resource stack deliberately small. More materials isn't better preparation — it's just more context switching.

Professor Messer's Network+ N10-009 course is the primary source. It's free, maps directly to the exam objectives, and is what most of the community points to as the baseline. The paid practice exams are part of the plan. Jason Dion's Udemy question bank for volume. ExamCompass for subnetting drills specifically — subnetting is the one area where raw repetition under time pressure matters more than conceptual understanding. Anki for spaced repetition on ports, protocols, and acronyms. The list is long and the forgetting curve is real.

What comes after

CCNA is next, and the goal is to have it done by end of summer. That's not a casual ambition — CCNA is a meaningfully harder exam than Network+, with deeper coverage of routing protocols, switching concepts, and Cisco-specific implementation. But the conversation in April made the timeline clear. The team is moving in a direction where that depth matters, and waiting until fall doesn't serve anyone.

After Network+ closes, the Ansible work picks back up — the Glances standardization playbook is still on the roadmap, and the roles refactor has been waiting. JNCIA-Junos gets a serious research pass once CCNA is in motion. And the lab will have new things to document. It always does.

But that's all for later. For now — heads down, exam close, journal quiet. See you on the other side.

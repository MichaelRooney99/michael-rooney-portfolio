Publishing Isn't the Same as Protecting

The homelab status page has run behind a Cloudflare Tunnel since the very first deploy, without me ever really explaining what that means. Setting up a new admin page this week meant configuring the tunnel side of things directly for the first time in a while, and it surfaced a distinction worth writing down — one that would be an easy, quiet mistake to make.

What a tunnel actually replaces

The traditional way to make something on your home network reachable from the internet is port forwarding — telling your router "traffic on port 443 goes to this specific machine inside." That works, but it means your router now has a real, permanent hole in it, sitting open and waiting, whether anyone's using it in a given moment or not. Every open port is a standing exposure.

A Cloudflare Tunnel flips the direction entirely. A small background process (cloudflared) running on your own infrastructure reaches out to Cloudflare and holds that connection open — the same shape as any outbound web request your browser makes every day. Nothing on your router ever needs to accept an incoming connection. When someone visits your public URL, Cloudflare routes their request down that already-open outbound tunnel to your machine. No open port, no NAT rule, no router config at all.

The gotcha: publishing isn't protecting

Here's the part that isn't obvious going in, and it's an easy thing to assume incorrectly: a tunnel route being "published" — reachable at a public URL — says nothing about who's allowed to reach it. By default, anyone on the internet who knows or guesses the URL can hit a published route. Access control is a genuinely separate layer, called Cloudflare Access, and it has to be configured on purpose. Skip that step and a route that feels private because it's "just a tunnel" is, in practice, exactly as open as a plain public website.

This mattered directly building the admin page this week — the write-capable part of the project, the first place anything here needed real access control. Publishing the route was step one. Locking it down was a deliberate, separate step two, done through a completely different part of Cloudflare's dashboard.

What Access actually adds

Cloudflare Access sits in front of a published route and checks every request against real identity before letting it through — not a password baked into the app, an actual login flow (email verification, or an OAuth provider) that happens at Cloudflare's edge, before the request ever reaches the origin server at all. The origin never has to know how to authenticate anyone; it just quietly trusts that anything reaching it already passed the check.

One detail worth knowing if you ever protect just part of a site rather than all of it: Access policies can be scoped to a specific path, not just an entire domain. The public status page and its new admin section share one domain — the policy only needed to apply to that one path prefix, leaving everything else exactly as open and unauthenticated as it already was. Get that path wrong, even slightly, and you either lock out the wrong thing or leave the actual sensitive part unprotected — worth double-checking, not assuming it's right on the first try.

The actual lesson

"It's on a private tunnel" and "it's protected" feel like they should mean the same thing, and they genuinely don't. A tunnel solves exposure — no open ports, no direct line to your router. It says nothing on its own about authorization — who's actually allowed through. Worth treating those as two separate questions every time, especially the moment anything write-capable gets added to a project that's otherwise been safely read-only.
